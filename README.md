# Mapping, Streets and Trails

**Notes on Two Public Online Identities from the Dharamshala Area**

From a Western vantage point, where environmental talk is often packaged for visibility and trail work frequently arrives with banners, sponsors and social-media documentation, the public online traces left by BholeKiBhasam and RustlingLittleBear stand out for their lack of performance.

### OpenStreetMap work

BholeKiBhasam first appeared on OpenStreetMap in 2014. Activity increased markedly in the last couple of years. The account’s diary entries give a clear sense of how the mapping is approached.

In the October 2026 entry titled “Mapping as a Escape, Reflection, and Connection Across the Map,” the writer describes shifting attention across continents after watching films about Aboriginal Australia: following tracks, reservoirs, cattle stations and unexpected peaks in the outback; then examining greenhouse horticulture in China as an example of careful land use; noting traditional dwellings of natural materials in Ethiopia alongside the advance of what is called “blinded modernization”; observing both hidden alpine lakes and large-scale deforestation in South America; recognising the disciplined farming and urban patterns of Japan and the Korean peninsula; and finally resting on the scale of Alaska and Siberia, which “ground you completely” and recall how quickly human development can be reclaimed.

Mapping on a mobile browser while travelling by public transport is mentioned as a practical way to ignore “visual pollution” — the billboards and roadside slogans. When real valleys and open landscapes appear, the phone is put away.

A second diary entry from mid-2026 questions the pursuit of high edit counts and “top contributor” rankings. The preference expressed is for precision over volume: a carefully drawn building or correctly aligned path is valued more than rapid, generalised polygons created mainly to increase numbers. The language remains matter-of-fact throughout.

### Street photography

The same geographic focus appears under the related public identity RustlingLittleBear. Photographs shared on Instagram, Behance and DeviantArt centre on the streets and hills around Dharamshala, McLeod Ganj and Bir Billing. Many are long-exposure images that turn moving people into soft traces, alongside ordinary market scenes, mountain light, monks and everyday details. The work is observational rather than staged.

### Independent trail work and stance on performative efforts

Visible in the same online spaces are records of small, independent trail clean-ups carried out without institutional banners, group photographs or organisational logos. Alongside these practical actions runs a consistent scepticism toward performative environmental efforts — the staged clean-up events that leave the same litter the following week, the gap between official or NGO language of “sustainable tourism” and the actual condition of the paths, and the tendency of some cooperatives and campaigns to prioritise visible activity over lasting change. The commentary is direct but not theatrical.

### Overall pattern

Taken together, the public material shows a steady pattern: detailed remote mapping treated as a form of quiet attention, street photography that records without spectacle, and local trail work done without an audience. There is no evident attempt to build a personal brand, collect followers for validation, or present the activity as exemplary. The accounts simply document what is being mapped, photographed and cleared.

For anyone interested in OpenStreetMap practice, Himalayan trail conditions, or the difference between performed and unperformed environmental attention, these public traces remain worth noticing precisely because they do not ask to be noticed.
